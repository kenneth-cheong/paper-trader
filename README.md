# Paper Trader

Practice trading Singapore (SGX) and US stocks with virtual cash at real market prices, and see whether you end up with a net profit or loss. Claude adds long/short picks based on the news, proposes strategies, and can run a fund of its own.

## What's in it

| Tab | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Home** | Your profit/loss, a chart of each account's value over time against the index, **AI picks** (which watchlist stocks to go long or short now, based on current news, with sources) with their track record, your holdings with where your money is and each holding's profit or loss, and the **reading guide**: what investing sites recommended for the watchlist's stocks and how their calls did, plus the articles you log yourself (see *Reading guide* below). Tap a stock's symbol for its **stock notes** (below). |
| **Markets** | Prices, day change and a 1-month sparkline for every stock, and a *results in N days* tag when a company reports soon. Buy, sell or short from here, or tap a symbol for its stock notes. |
| **Rule-based** | Rules that trade for you: limit buys, stop-losses, take-profits, trailing stops, moving-average crossovers and scheduled buys. Each rule can be backtested on the past year. |
| **AI strategist** | Claude reads a year of prices, current news and your own trades, then proposes strategies written as trading rules. Each strategy is backtested automatically, and you can add it as paused rules. |
| **AI fund** | Give Claude an amount and it trades on its own to make as much profit as possible, without ever using more than that amount. Run up to 5 funds side by side, each with its own style, focus and model, and compare them on a chart. Each fund's page opens with *This week*, its weekly report of what it learned. Charts show its value, positions, what it has learned and the AI's monthly cost; a *Stops and risk* table shows how much of its daily risk each position carries. |
| **History** | Realized profit over time, and every trade, with automatic ones marked. |

### Accounts and trading rules

- **Two virtual cash accounts:** SGD 100,000 for SGX stocks and USD 100,000 for US stocks. You can change both amounts when you reset. Profit and loss is counted per currency, so exchange-rate moves don't affect it. A combined SGD figure is shown for reference.
- **Net profit/loss** = cash + value of holdings − starting cash. It's split into realized (closed trades) and unrealized (open positions).
- **Short selling:** selling more than you hold opens a short. Like a margin account, each short sets aside 150% of its sale value from your buying power until you buy it back ("cover").
- **Trading fees** are charged on every trade (see *Trading fees* below). Whole shares only. While a market is trading, orders fill at the latest fetched price. **Orders placed while it's closed wait** (listed under Holdings, where you can cancel them) and fill at the first price after it reopens, as with a real broker.
- **Market hours:** SGX 9:00am–12:00pm and 1:00pm–5:00pm Singapore time; US 9:30am–4:00pm New York time (daylight saving is handled). A market only counts as open when the clock says so *and* today's prices are arriving, so public holidays and early closes show as closed with no holiday calendar to maintain.
- **Converting currency:** the *Convert SGD ↔ USD* button on the combined card moves cash between your accounts at the latest rate, less your fee plan's conversion spread (Tiger about 0.1%, Standard Chartered about 0.5%: estimates; set your own under *My own rates*). Each account's profit and loss counts money converted in or out, so only the spread shows as a cost.
- **Dividends and splits** are applied automatically. A split changes your share count, not what you paid (10 shares at $300 become 30 at $100). Dividends are credited on the ex-date for shares held before it: US dividends lose 30% to US withholding tax (the rate for a Singapore resident), SGX dividends are paid in full, and a short pays the dividend. They show on the account cards and in History.
- **Index comparison:** each account card shows what the same starting money would have made in an index fund over the same period (SPY for USD, ES3 for SGD), after the buying fee.
- **Your portfolio and rules** are saved to your account when accounts are on (see *Accounts* below); otherwise they live in your browser, and *Settings → Export* backs them up or moves them to another device.

### Trading fees

Every trade pays fees, so all profit and loss is **after fees**:
- **Buying:** the fee is added to what the shares cost you, so it's in your average price.
- **Selling:** the fee is taken from what you receive.
- **Where you see them:** the trade window shows the fees before you confirm, each account card shows *Fees paid*, and History lists the fee for every trade.
- **Also included in:** backtests, rule-based trading, orders placed while a market is closed, and the AI fund.

Choose the fees in *Settings → Trading fees*:

| Plan | US stocks | SGX stocks |
|---|---|---|
| **Tiger Brokers (Singapore)** (default) | US$0.005/share commission (min US$0.99, max 0.5%) + US$0.005/share platform fee (min US$1, max 0.5%); SEC and FINRA fees on sales | 0.03% commission (min S$0.99) + 0.03% platform fee (min S$1) + SGX clearing 0.0325% and trading 0.0075% |
| **Standard Chartered Online Trading** | 0.20%, US$10 minimum (check your rate) | 0.20%, S$10 minimum + SGX fees |
| **My own rates** | a percentage with a minimum | a percentage with a minimum |
| **No fees** | – | – |

9% GST is added to the broker's and SGX's fees. Priority and accredited Standard Chartered customers pay less, so use *My own rates* if that's you. These are published retail rates as of September 2026 and can change.

**Examples:** US$1,000 of a US stock costs about US$2.17 in fees with Tiger. S$38,000 of an SGX stock costs about S$41 with Tiger and S$99 with Standard Chartered.

The **AI fund** uses the Tiger, Standard Chartered or no-fee plan you choose when starting it. When trading through Tiger, it records the fees Tiger actually charged, using the Tiger plan only as an estimate until Tiger reports them. The AI is told what a round trip costs, so it avoids trades too small to cover their fees.

### Charts

Each account's **value over time** is rebuilt from your trades at each day's closing prices, with the money you put in (dashed; it steps when you convert currency) and what the same money would be worth in the index (grey; it gets the same money in and out). Other charts show where your money is, each holding's profit or loss, the AI picks' track record against a coin flip, realized profit over time, and on the AI fund page the funds side by side, what its ideas did a week later, each lesson's number week by week (a small trend line under the lesson, in the weekly report and under *What it has learned*), the market memory, what followed big moves with and without news, each ten-year pattern on the years it was found on and the years held out, and the AI's monthly spend against the cap; on the Home page, the reading guide's record of each investing site.

Each stock's notes have a chart of its past year with its results days (green where it beat its index that day, red where it lagged), its ex-dividend dates and the fund's (or your) trades marked on the line.

Hover, tap or drag a chart for exact numbers; line charts also take keyboard focus (the arrow keys step through the days, Escape closes the tooltip). Every chart has a **Table** switch with the same numbers.

For anyone changing them: the charts are hand-drawn SVG in `charts.js` with no library. Colours come from CSS variables in `styles.css` (`--series-1` to `--series-8` for identity, in a fixed order checked for colour-blind separation in light and dark mode; green and red only where a value is good or bad). Marks are thin (bars at most 14px, 2px lines), axis ticks are round numbers, labels are measured so nothing is cut off (on phones, bar labels move above their bars), there's never a second y-axis, and every chart needs a table twin.

### AI picks' track record

Every scheduled set of picks is kept with each stock's price when it was picked. Under the picks, the **track record** scores each one after a week and a month of trading: whether it made money in its direction (a short gains when the price falls), and whether it beat the index over the same days (the S&P 500 for US stocks, the STI for SGX). Dividends count, for the stock and the index (a long receives them, after US withholding tax for US stocks; a short pays them). Before fees. Picks older than the year of daily prices drop out of the record. If the picks can't beat the index, don't follow them.

### Rule-based trading

Rules run whenever the page is open. When you come back after being away, they replay the last 5 trading days of 15-minute prices, so a rule still fires at the time and price it would have. If you're away longer than that, the gap is skipped. Rules only manage long positions; they never open shorts.

### The AI fund's hard limit

The fund's ledger starts with exactly the amount you give it, and nothing is ever added. The code, not the AI, rejects any order that costs more than the fund's buying power. Shorts need 150% collateral and are covered automatically at a 40% loss, so the fund can't lose more than its amount. The fund trades one market, set by the currency you choose: USD for US stocks, SGD for SGX stocks. It can hold long and short positions and set its own stop-loss and take-profit levels, which are checked every 15 minutes. It only makes decisions while its market is actually trading, never on a holiday or after an early close.

The fund page compares the fund with **the same money in the index** (SPY or ES3, bought when the fund started, after fees), draws the index as a grey line on the value chart, and shows **what the AI has cost** next to the profit after that cost. Dividends and splits on its holdings are applied as for your portfolio.

## How it runs

A GitHub Actions workflow (`.github/workflows/prices.yml`) runs every 15 minutes on weekdays while SGX or US markets are open. Each run:

1. fetches two years of daily prices (with each day's trading volume) and 5 days of 15-minute prices for every stock in `symbols.json` from Yahoo Finance's public chart endpoint (no key needed), two years of the VIX and a year of the US dollar's daily rate in Singapore dollars. `data/prices.json`, which the page and every other step read, keeps the last year of each (the VIX apart from the watchlist, under `macro`, for the *Regime today* line; `fx` is still the latest exchange rate); the two years of daily open, high, low, close and volume go to `data/ohlcv.json`, for the AI fund step's factor lab only (see *Its style, and when its ideas work*): it stays on the runner and is never published;
2. checks the **results calendar**: US results releases from SEC filings (at most every 2 hours, if set up), and once a day, after the US close, each company's next results date and analysts' views from Yahoo Finance (see *Results calendar and analysts* below);
3. once a week, downloads **ten years of daily prices** for the watchlist and the VIX and rebuilds the ten-year market memory (see *How the AI funds learn*), and once a day refreshes the **stock notes** (see *Stock notes* below);
4. at most every 6 hours, reads the **news feeds** (Singapore and US business news, and Yahoo Finance's headlines for each stock) and keeps the headlines that name a watchlist stock, as leads for the news digest, then checks the day's big moves for news (see *News feeds* below); once a day it also reads the new headlines' calls for the **reading guide** (see *Reading guide* below);
5. refreshes the **AI picks** if they're more than 10 hours old, which works out to about twice each trading day;
6. runs the **AI fund**: it checks stop-loss, take-profit and forced-cover levels, then asks Claude for a decision when one is due. Decisions are spread through the market's trading day, starting 15 minutes after the open. After the last session of each fund's market in a week, it writes that fund's **weekly report** (see *How the AI funds learn*), which Telegram sends too; then it answers any questions you've asked in **Ask the data** (see below), downloading the ten years of prices again if a question waits and the weekly step didn't run;
7. saves the AI picks, the fund, the calendar, the ten-year memory, the stock notes, the news feeds' headlines and the reading guide's calls to the `ai-state` branch, so `main`'s history stays clean;
8. redeploys the site to GitHub Pages.

The page reloads everything every 5 minutes. Prices can be 20–30 minutes old, because Yahoo delays SGX quotes and GitHub sometimes starts scheduled runs late. Exchange holidays aren't known, so on a holiday the market simply shows no new prices.

**A dependable timer:** GitHub starts scheduled workflows late and, at busy times, skips them altogether (a new repository can wait hours for its first scheduled run). Once the AI fund controls are set up (the GitHub token in Supabase Vault, below), run `supabase/schedule.sql` in Supabase's SQL Editor: Supabase then asks GitHub for a run on the same timetable. When both timers fire, the job still runs once.

## Setup

1. Create a **public** GitHub repository, e.g. `paper-trader`, and push this folder to its `main` branch. GitHub Pages is free only for public repos. Your own trades stay in your browser, but AI picks and the AI fund are published on the site.
2. **Settings → Pages → Build and deployment → Source:** choose **GitHub Actions**.
3. **Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions → New repository secret:** add `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` with your key from [console.anthropic.com](https://console.anthropic.com). This powers the scheduled AI picks and the AI fund. Set a spending limit on the key.
4. Optional: choose the models on the same page's **Variables** tab (see *Which model does what* below), and add `SEC_USER_AGENT` there for exact US results dates (see *Results calendar and analysts*).
5. **Actions → Update prices, AI picks and AI fund → Run workflow.** When it finishes, the site is at `https://<your-username>.github.io/paper-trader/`.

For the **AI strategist** and the **Refresh now** button on AI picks, add your API key in the app with the **Settings & API keys** button (top right) → *Anthropic API key*. It is stored only in that browser and sent straight to Anthropic. The same dialog's *Connections* list shows whether each connection (your browser key, the scheduled AI jobs' key, Tiger, your account) is working. Tiger's keys are never typed into the app: the site is public, so they live only in GitHub secrets (below).

**The AI strategist without a key in the browser (admins):** when accounts are on and an admin has no key saved in the browser, *Analyse* goes to the scheduled job instead, like the AI fund buttons (so it needs `supabase/fund-control.sql` and the token in Vault). The app sends your part of the strategist's data (fees, accounts, holdings, active rules and last 40 trades, about 2 KB) as the workflow's command, which the job reads from the runner's event file, never printing it. The job adds the stocks from its fresh prices and the AI picks' news digest when it's under 4 hours old, runs the same analysis with the `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` secret, and keeps the latest answer with the AI funds (private in Supabase; the public copy leaves it out). It appears on the AI strategist tab in about 3–5 minutes, and its cost (about US$0.05–0.15, more when it has to gather its own news) is in the spend ledger as *AI strategist* and stops at the monthly cap. A key in the browser still answers straight away.

### Several AI funds

Up to **5 funds** can run at once. Each has its own amount, currency (market), broker (simulator or Tiger), approval setting, decisions per day (see below), limits and:

- **Style**, which is the AI's brief and sets the default limits (you can still change them):
  - **Cautious:** large, established companies, gradual positions, a stop-loss on everything, no shorts. 10% max per order, pauses after a 3% daily loss.
  - **Balanced:** a spread of positions and sensible risk. 25% per order, 5% daily loss, shorts allowed.
  - **Aggressive:** concentrated bets on the strongest ideas, momentum and shorts. 40% per order, 8% daily loss.
- **Focus** (optional), your own instruction, for example *Only Singapore banks and REITs* or *Big US tech*.
- **AI model**: the default (Sonnet), or Haiku (cheapest) or Opus (strongest, most expensive).

The AI fund page ranks the funds by return after the AI's cost, and shows each one's return against the index. Tap a fund to see its positions, orders and decisions, and to pause, stop or change it. *Pause all funds* is the kill switch for all of them. A stopped fund stays listed until you remove it; a one-line summary of its result is kept.

Funds trading through Tiger share your one Tiger account. Each fund keeps its own record, and the check that Tiger holds what the funds think covers all of them together. One account can't be long and short the same stock, so a fund can't open a position on the other side of another Tiger fund's position in that stock. Each fund's decisions cost separately, and the monthly AI cap is shared.

### How the AI funds learn

Each fund keeps a **playbook** of lessons from its own results, shown on its page under *What it has learned* and given to the AI at every decision.

- **Every idea is graded, not just trades:** trades it made, trades you declined, proposals that expired, orders its limits blocked, stocks it considered but passed on (it lists up to 3 each decision), and its exits, stop-losses and take-profits. A week, a month and a quarter (63 trading days) later, the code measures what the price did, in the idea's direction and against the index. For an exit, it checks whether the stock kept going the position's way (sold too early?), and for stop-losses whether the price recovered (stop too tight?).
- **Graded fairly:** moves include dividends, for the stock and the index, as the fund's cash does (US dividends after the 30% withholding tax on a long; a short pays them), so a buy isn't marked down just because the stock went ex-dividend. The same idea repeated within 5 trading days (say, passing on NVDA at every decision for a week) counts as one idea, keeping its first time and price, so one bet can't make a lesson on its own. The same applies when several funds' ideas are pooled. An idea from before the year of daily prices is left out rather than graded on the wrong days.
- **Nothing learned is lost:** once an idea's month grade is final, it's kept in a compact log with the fund (the latest 2,000 ideas, about 100–180 bytes each, with its thesis and, for an entry, its factors; its quarter grade is added when that's in), so lessons survive old decisions being trimmed (the fund keeps its last 500 real decisions; skipped ones don't count towards that) and the year of prices moving on. The public copy of the fund keeps only the latest 200 of these, without each idea's thesis, factors and quarter grade, or the reason you declined it.
- **Beta, fees and peers taken out:** a fund that owns stocks which swing more than the index beats it in a rising week without any skill. So each result is split into three parts: the market's part (the stock's *beta*, how much it has moved with its index over the year before the idea, times the index's move), fees, and what's left, the **stock-specific edge**. Every entry idea is charged a round trip of fees: what a simulator fill actually paid, or for Tiger orders, declined, expired, blocked and passed-on ideas, the fund's fee plan at the order's size (a typical order for ideas passed on). Exits aren't charged again. Each idea is also compared with its peers: the other stocks in its market, and for DBS, OCBC and UOB the other two banks.
- **Separate bets, not cases:** ideas on the same stock and side whose weeks overlap count as one bet, and the uncertainty is worked out week by week, so one market-wide selloff counts once. The fund's universe is small and correlated, so there are far fewer separate bets than ideas.
- **Lessons by rule, only when the evidence is strong:** for example, *ideas you passed on did better than the market explains (119 separate bets, edge +0.8% a week, likely +0.4% to +1.2%)*, *your momentum trades beat the index only because of beta*, *your DBS buys beat the index but lagged OCBC and UOB: right on banks, wrong bank*. Each lesson's edge is an estimate pulled towards zero (a stock-specific edge is rarely beyond ±0.5% a week), with a likely range (an 8-in-10 chance it's inside) and a confidence. A lesson needs at least 8 separate bets, an edge of 0.3% a week or more and a 97% chance that its sign is right; once shown, it stays until that chance falls below 75%, so lessons don't flicker (only a lesson that passed that full test once; see below for older ones). That 97% was set by a check on pure noise (`noiseCheck` in `learning.js`, run by the tests): on 200 made-up funds with no skill, re-checked weekly for half a year, 2% ended up with more than one false lesson and 18% with one (at 90%, a quarter had two or more). The fund's page prints this rate. Expect mostly *Watching* for the first months: patterns on their way (a 70% chance or more) are listed for you only, with about how many more bets they need, and the AI doesn't see them. The AI and the page see each lesson's confidence (*High* from 99%, *Moderate*, or *Fading* while a lesson loses its evidence), its number per week with its likely range, and separate bets; the number says what it measures: the stock-specific edge for most lessons, the result against the index for a *beta-driven* one (whose edge is near zero), against its peers for *right on banks, wrong bank*, or high minus low conviction. Lessons shown before this change keep the old measure (against the index only) for 4 weeks; after that they have to pass the full test like any new lesson. This costs nothing: no AI is involved.
- **A short thesis on every idea, graded for calibration:** with every order and every idea it passes on, the AI says what move it expects in its direction before fees (0 for a sell), over a week, a month or a quarter; the catalyst (results, guidance, a dividend, a rate decision, economic data, a deal, a product, valuation, a chart signal, or none) and its date; what would prove it wrong; and which of its lessons it applied (up to 3, by id). The stop-loss and take-profit it sets are the thesis's exit levels. The code checks two things when the order is made and only notes them: whether the catalyst is more than 10 trading days old (for results, against the results calendar, so last quarter's results count as old), and whether the expected move covers the round-trip fee at that size. Later, each thesis is graded at its own horizon, and the **Calibration** table on the fund's page shows expected against realised by conviction, horizon and catalyst, with the result against the index at 5, 21 and 63 trading days (horizon fit). Whether the catalyst came within the horizon is checked only where code can tell: ex-dividend dates, and results dates where the calendar covers the idea's window (the results before it and the next ones after it are both known), else it's left unknown. Fixed rules turn this into lessons, for example *your buys expected +6.2% over about a month and realised +0.9% (22 separate bets, pooled across the SGD funds): expect less, and size smaller*, *ideas you gave a week paid at a month*, *ideas whose catalyst was 10+ trading days old lagged the index by 2.2%*, *in 7 of 30 orders the expected move didn't cover the round-trip fee*, or *your high-conviction ideas have done worse than your low-conviction ones*. For shorts the words follow the price: *your shorts expected a 4.2% fall and got a 1.6% rise*. Ideas on the same stock and side count as one bet while their windows overlap (a week, a month or a quarter: an idea's own horizon). Judging expected against realised needs 10 separate bets and a gap of 2 points; comparing kinds of ideas needs 8 bets each. Every rule that judges results also needs the gap to be clearly beyond noise (a 99% chance on a t-distribution), measured after the market's part (beta times the index's move) and judged by the calendar weeks the bets started in, so one market-wide selloff that hit ten stocks bought the same week counts once. The same check on pure noise (`calibrationNoiseCheck`, run by the tests) makes up 200 funds whose expected moves were honest, re-checks the rules weekly for half a year and prints the rate on the page: about 11% showed one false calibration lesson and none more than one. The same AI runs every fund, so these are also pooled across the funds in a market (the fund's own lesson comes first). If the expected moves hardly vary (by less than 1 point, e.g. always +5%), the lesson says they're formulaic instead of judging them. At most 3 calibration lessons reach the AI, and you can remove them like any other. Each position on the fund's page shows its thesis, e.g. *expects +6% in a month · results 23 Oct · so far +1.2% · wrong if NIM guidance is cut*, and each decision shows the lessons it applied (a lesson that has since gone is shown with the wording the AI saw). The thesis fields add roughly US$0.40 a month to the AI decisions; grading them costs nothing.
- **Weekly review, checked in code:** about once a week, after the market closes and only when there are at least 5 newly graded ideas, Claude Haiku reads cells worked out in code (for each group of ideas, e.g. trades based on news, or ideas passed on in DBS: the ideas, separate bets, the stock-specific edge a week later with its standard error, and whether it's clear), the most telling examples and the lesson book (below), and writes up to 6 sharper lessons. Each lesson says which ideas it's about (a filter: the outcome, kind of idea, side, stock, conviction, horizon and catalyst, any of them "any") and whether they did better or worse than the market explains. The code then checks each one on those ideas: it replaces the review's numbers with its own, and drops a lesson with fewer than 8 separate bets or that the data doesn't back (under a 3-in-4 chance that it's right, the level at which a rule-made lesson is withdrawn). A lesson about all ideas can't be checked, so it's shown as an *opinion, not checked* and expires after 4 weeks unless you keep it. A lesson's id comes from its filter and claim, so the same lesson written again next week, in any words, is the same lesson, and one you removed stays removed. One on the same ideas with the same claim as a rule-made lesson or one of yours is already in force, so it isn't added a second time; and a review lesson gives way when the rule-made lesson on the same ideas comes on (if you had removed it, the rule-made one stays removed too). Lessons stay until you remove them (up to 6 from the reviews at a time; beyond that the ones not written again for longest make room, never one you kept or one that didn't hold). The review also writes three plain sentences for you (what the graded ideas showed and what the coming weeks should tell: counts and trends, no advice and no questions; not which lessons it wrote, since code checks those after it and the report says which it kept), which go in the weekly report. Each review costs about 1 US cent or less.
- **Lessons you can check:** every lesson with a filter (the rule-made ones, the weekly review's and yours) is followed on the ideas that came after it was learned. The fund's page shows under each: when it was learned and on how many separate bets, what the ideas since have done, and a status: *too early to tell* (under 8 new separate bets), *held on new data* (a 9-in-10 chance that it's right on them), *didn't hold on new data* (that chance the other way) or *not clear yet*. A lesson that compares its ideas in another way (calibration's, or *beta-driven*) and one of yours without a filter say *not checked on new data*; one of yours with a filter shows what it's tracked as (e.g. *ideas passed on do better than the market explains*), since that decides whether it held. On made-up funds with no skill (`holdNoiseCheck` in `learning.js`, run by the tests), about 2% of lessons showed as held and 3% as didn't hold, by chance, while a real edge of 1% a week never showed as didn't hold, and on ideas that come up a few times a week (like ideas passed on) it showed as held on almost every made-up fund within 9 months; rarer kinds of idea take longer. Nothing is removed automatically, and nothing fades with time: a lesson that didn't hold asks whether you want to remove it. It also shows whether the AI changed what it does, without trusting what it says: the share of its ideas matching the lesson before and after it was learned, with the counts (e.g. *trades based on news: 49% of its trades before, 19% since*), and how often its decisions cited it. A track record sums it up: *of the 6 lessons it has had that can be checked, 2 held on new data, 2 didn't and 2 aren't clear yet*. The AI sees whether a lesson held or didn't, and which are opinions.
- **You're in charge:** remove any lesson (and restore it later), keep an opinion from expiring, or add your own, from the fund's page. Your own lesson can say which of its ideas it's about (*Track it*: dropdowns for the same filter), so it's tracked like the others; the AI sees only your words. Lessons never override the hard limits.
- **The weekly report, "What we learned":** on the first run after the last session of each fund's market in a week (Friday, half an hour after the close; on a Friday holiday, as soon as it's clear the market isn't trading; a week the job missed is caught up the next week), whether or not the weekly review ran, the job writes up the fund's week. It opens the fund's page as *This week*, with a picker for the last 12 weeks, and Telegram sends it as a message of its own with a link to that fund's page. It tells the week with counts and trends, not verdicts:
  - the fund against its index that week, and against a fund of the same currency and style with learning switched the other way, if you run one (this week, and since both were running);
  - the ideas whose week grade came in that week, with the best and the worst against the index after fees, in the words of their reasons and what the AI said would prove them wrong;
  - what changed in its lessons since the week before, e.g. *"Your shorts have lost money…" got stronger: 5 → 6 separate bets, −3.1% → −3.8% a week (still Moderate)*; new lessons, lessons that went (and why), and lessons that held or didn't on new data. Each lesson's evidence is kept week by week (the last 12 weeks), and the page draws it as a small trend line under the lesson, here and under *What it has learned*, with a table of the same numbers. A lesson got stronger or weaker only when the figures printed (to a tenth of a point) differ; otherwise it held steady;
  - what the weekly review did, in a week it ran: the lessons it added or wrote again, and those the check dropped, with why (e.g. *fewer than 8 separate bets*);
  - the weekly review's three sentences, in a week it ran (a quiet week's are kept for the next full report, dated, for up to two weeks);
  - your calls (below), in a week when a trade you declined was graded;
  - coming up: results and ex-dividend dates in the next 30 days for what it holds or has waiting for your approval (from the stock notes, whose results dates come from the results calendar), with the price's usual ex-date drop;
  - the month's AI cost: its own decisions, and learning (the weekly reviews and news look-ups, shared by every fund) from the spend ledger.
  With fewer than 5 ideas graded that week, the report is one line saying so. It costs nothing: no AI is involved beyond the review's three sentences.
- **Your calls:** on a Tiger fund where you approve each trade, *Reject* asks why: *too risky*, *bad timing*, *don't like the stock* or *other*. The reason is kept with the trade and graded like the fund's own ideas, and the fund's page shows the record in *Your calls*: for each reason, how many of the trades you declined would have lost money a week and a month later after fees (you were right), and what they'd have made against the index (for a sale you declined, you were right when the stock kept going the position's way). Once you've used a reason 5 times, the AI sees it as your preferences and record (e.g. *too risky: 7 declined, 5 would have lost money a week later*), never as a rule: about 40 tokens a decision while it does, plus one sentence in its instructions and the review's three sentences: about US$0.03 a month in all. The reason reaches the job as a *settings* request, so `supabase/fund-control.sql` doesn't need re-running.
- **Market memory**, shared by every fund in a market: how its stocks moved after good or bad company news, and after one-day moves of 4% or more, over the past year, measured from prices. Every daily news summary adds to it at no extra cost. To fill in the past year straight away, run **Actions → Update prices, AI picks and AI fund → Run workflow** with *Learning: look up the past year of news* ticked. That is a one-off costing about US$1–2 (Claude Haiku searches for each stock's main news of the year); running it again only fills gaps. The AI only supplies what happened; the price moves are measured in code, dividends included. News with an exact release time counts from the first session that could react to it: US results out after the 4pm close count from the next day. Where a primary source exists, it replaces what the AI recalled: US results come from the companies' SEC filings (with their exact release time; good or bad by the earnings surprise, and an in-line result within ±2% is left out), and rating changes from Yahoo Finance's dated list. A stock has at most one news event per trading day and tone: the same story worded differently by two digests, or a filing and a digest item on the same results day, is one price move, not two. The one kept is an SEC filing, else the AI's news (results first: they drive the day), else a Yahoo rating change (which mostly follows the day's news), else a news feed's headline. An SGX news date from the AI only counts if that day traded at least 1.8 times the usual volume (the median of the previous 20 sessions); if only the next day did, the news came after the close and counts from that day; otherwise it moves to such a day within 3 sessions, or is dropped. A results date at a quarter's end (the last week of March, June, September or December) is the period's end, not the news, so it's left out even if a nearby volume spike (a rebalance, an ex-date) could have moved it; only an exactly timed release is trusted as it is. An idea made during an ex-dividend session and priced at the day before's close counts that dividend, for the stock and the index. Its lessons go through the same evidence engine as the fund's own: the drift after allowing for each stock's beta, in separate bets (the same stock moving the same way within a week is one), with the uncertainty worked out by date, so one market-wide selloff that hit every stock counts once. It may still favour memorable events, so treat these as tendencies, not rules.
- **Big moves with and without news**, on each market memory panel: each time a stock moves 4% or more against its index in a day (its total return beyond the index's), the move is judged once, after the next session, on whether any news came out within a trading day of it: a digest's or the backfill's news item, an SEC filing, a rating change, or a news feed headline naming the stock (see *News feeds* below). For a move with none, Claude Haiku searches the web once (at most 10 a month, about 2 US cents each, in the spend ledger as *article look-ups*; a search that fails after the API answered is still billed, so it's counted, uses up one of the 10 and isn't tried again, and one that never reached the API is tried once more); news it finds with a source it read, dated within a trading day of the move, joins the market memory's news events. The panel compares what followed moves with news and moves without: the next week and month in the move's direction, against the index and after beta, with how many moves, how many had no news, and how many searches found some. Whether a move had news is decided before any search, so the search can't move a case from one group to the other; and the comparison starts on the day the news feeds first answered, because before them far fewer moves would have had news on record. It's descriptive, with few cases for months: no lesson comes from it, and the AI doesn't see it. The moves are kept in `state/move-news.json` (the stock, day and size of each, whether it had news, and what its search found), and the *Market memory report* workflow prints the comparison.
- **Ten years of prices**, shared by every fund in a market, and checked on years it wasn't found on. Once a week (when the last build is 7 or more days old) the job downloads ten years of daily prices for every stock in `symbols.json`, with their dividends and splits, plus the VIX: about 21 requests, which stay on the runner and are never saved. The studies themselves use no AI.
  - **Studies fixed in advance**, per market: what followed a big one-day move up or down (at least 4% and 2.5 times the stock's usual daily move, the spread of its last 60 days' moves; the report also splits them by heavy volume, 2 times the 50-day average); last week's best stock against its worst, the following week; the ex-dividend dates of the SGX banks, C38U and Singtel (the drop against the dividend, and the 21 trading days after); and US results (the drift after good and bad results, dated from SEC filings where there are some, else Yahoo's past dates, with each stock's typical results-day move).
  - **Measured fairly:** each outcome is taken 5 and 21 trading days on, beyond the market's part (beta times the index, as for every lesson) and the stock's own usual drift over the year before (today's watchlist is made of winners; this takes most of their drift out). The same stock and side within the window is one separate bet, and a market-wide day counts once.
  - **The check:** a pattern is found on 2016–2023 (a 90% chance of its sign, 8 or more separate bets, and at least 0.3% over its horizon, as every lesson needs) and must then show the same sign, at half its size or more, on 2024 onwards, which it wasn't found on. Otherwise the lesson the AI gets is that there's no reliable pattern, which stops it assuming a rebound or momentum. On pure noise (`holdoutNoiseCheck` in `memory-long.js`, run by the tests) about 2% of made-up studies pass, and the page prints the rate; with about 8 studies, expect a false pattern now and then. Results dates only go back as far as the SEC filings and Yahoo's dates do (a few years), so that study says it has too few cases before 2024 until they build up.
  - **Stop-loss base rates:** for a position opened at any close, how often ordinary swings touched a stop 1.5, 2, 2.5 or 3 typical daily moves away within 21 trading days (the day's low for a long and its high for a short, since stops trigger during the day, ex-dividend drops included), and the stop they reached in only 1 hold in 5, stock by stock.
  - **The data is checked first:** Yahoo's dividend-adjusted closes are compared with its dividends, and where they disagree (Thai Beverage's start at 0.0016 against a close of 0.965) the total return is rebuilt from closes and dividends; a split Yahoo didn't apply to the older closes is applied; one-day moves over 30% (a bad print, or a restructuring like Keppel's) and runs of 5 or more unchanged closes (a suspension) are left out of every study, but never the VIX's swings.
  - **Where it goes:** the results (`state/memory-long.json`, under 50 KB) go to the `ai-state` branch and the site, where each fund's page shows its market's ten-year panel. The AI sees these lessons with the past year's (whose big-move lessons give way to the ten-year ones), at most 6 in all, those whose evidence covers days like today first, in shorter words (every "no reliable pattern" as one lesson): about 200–400 tokens a decision, a few US cents a month. If Yahoo fails, or sends back less than last week (a stock or a whole market missing, or a stock's prices years shorter), last week's results are kept and the download is tried again 6 hours later; after 4 tries (a day) the new build is taken, with the last figures for what's still missing carried over for up to 4 weeks, and the panel says which.
  - **Regime today**, on the Markets tab, on each ten-year panel and in the market data the AI reads: whether the index (SPY or ES3) is above or below its 200-day average and, for US stocks, whether the VIX is calm (under 16), normal or stressed (over 25); a VIX that stopped updating isn't used once it's over 4 days older than the index's price. No ten-year lesson depends on it (a lesson from *When its ideas work*, below, can); the *Market memory report* workflow prints each study by regime, and how many separate stress episodes each period held.
  - These are measured on 17 of today's stocks, which survived and mostly won: tendencies, not laws.
- **Its style, and when its ideas work (the factor lab)**, on each fund's page. Every order and every idea it passes on keeps six numbers about its stock, fixed in advance and measured from the daily bars that had closed before it (never the session still trading): the price against its 50-day average, how far it is below its 52-week high, the last session's volume against the average of the 20 before it, the last month's move divided by the stock's usual month (the spread of its last 60 daily moves), trading days to its next results or since its last (from the results calendar), and the index (SPY or ES3) against its 200-day average; with the VIX and, for SGX stocks, the US dollar's move against the Singapore dollar over the month. They're a compact array on the order (`factors.js`); ideas from before this was kept get theirs from the two years of prices in `data/ohlcv.json`, and every graded idea keeps them as columns of the frozen idea log (old rows keep working, and get theirs added while the prices still cover them).
  - **Its revealed style:** where the stocks it chose sat among its market's stocks on each of those numbers at the time (the average percentile: 0 the lowest that day, 100 the highest), against what it called its ideas, e.g. *its buys sat at the 78th percentile of its market's stocks on the month's move for its volatility, 71st on price against its 50-day average and 4.2% below their 52-week high on average; 25% were opened in the 5 trading days before results. It called 45% of them value ideas.* From the trades it made or proposed (not the ideas it passed on), with a chart of the percentiles and its table.
  - **When its ideas work:** each market's funds' trades pooled (the same AI runs them all), one case per fund, stock and side per 5 trading days, split into buckets fixed in advance for each number (e.g. *within 5% of the 52-week high*, *in the 5 trading days before results*, *VIX calm*), each with its cases, separate bets, the calendar weeks and separate episodes they came from (spells of that condition; for results, separate stocks' quarters), and the stock-specific edge a week later (after beta and fees, each week counted once) with its likely range. Buckets with fewer than 8 separate bets are greyed, in the chart and its table. It's descriptive: a bucket is often one or two stocks, and a regime can last months.
  - **Only three comparisons can become lessons**, fixed in advance: ideas opened in the 5 trading days before results against the rest, the index below its 200-day average against above it, and a stressed VIX (over 25) against a calm one (under 16; US funds, as for the regime line). Each side needs 20 or more separate bets from 6 or more calendar weeks and 2 or more separate episodes, the difference at least 2.5 times its standard error counted by week (on the page: 2.5 times what noise alone would usually give), and the same sign in both halves of the record. The lesson is about the side that did worse (e.g. *with SPY below its 200-day average, its ideas have done worse than when SPY was above it: −1.2% a week against +0.4%*), and the AI sees it only while that side's condition holds today (the regime now, or one of its market's stocks reporting within 5 trading days), marked *applies_now*; the page says when. On pure noise (`conditionNoiseCheck` in `factors.js`, run by the tests), 200 made-up markets re-checked every week for a year, 8% showed a false lesson at some point, and the page prints the rate. Nothing else here reaches the AI, except one new number in its price statistics: each stock's last session's volume against its 20-day average (`rel_volume_20d`). No factor-by-regime scans, and no other series.
  - **Where it goes:** each market's lab is in the funds' collection (`c.factorLab`, a few KB), each fund's style in its playbook; the public copy of the idea log leaves the factor columns out. It costs nothing: no AI, a few more tokens for the volume number, and no extra requests (still one daily request per stock, now for two years).
- **Is it working?** Untick *Learns from its results* on one fund to keep it as a control. The leaderboard then shows whether learning helps. It also shows each fund's **beta** (how much its value has moved with its index day to day, from its value after each session) once it has 40 trading days: a fund with a beta above 1 should beat a rising index without any skill, and one below 0 (net short) moves against it.

**Saving AI cost:**
- A fund **skips a decision** when nothing has changed since its last one: no new news or picks, no fills, its stocks within 2.5% and the index within 1%. It skips at most twice in a row. You can turn this off per fund.
- Funds in the same market deciding in the same run on the same model **share a cached copy** of the market data (news, picks and price statistics, most of the prompt). The extra funds pay a tenth of the normal price for it.
- Costs shown count cached input at its real, cheaper rate.

### Results calendar and analysts

Results days are when a stock is most likely to jump or fall overnight, so the app tracks them, and the news the AI reads says how old each story is.

- **What the AI sees:** the stocks reporting in the next 10 trading days, with how much each one's results days have moved it against the index on average (for example *NVDA reports in 2 trading days; its results days moved ±7.8% against SPY*), and any position the fund holds that reports within 3 trading days, with its share of the fund. It's told to size positions for that move. Where Yahoo has them, each stock also shows how many analysts rate it buy, hold or sell, their average price target and how far that is from the price, and the rating changes of the last 10 trading days with the move since.
- **Old news is marked as old:** every news item carries its age, and items more than 10 trading days old are passed as background, not as a fresh reason to trade (the news search sometimes brings back months-old results).
- **On the page:** a *results in N days* tag on the Markets list, your holdings and each fund's positions ("estimate" when the company hasn't confirmed the date; hover for the source), and a *Broker rating changes* list under the market memory, with how each stock has moved since. Only real upgrades and downgrades are listed, not ratings kept or new price targets.
- **Telegram:** the evening before a stock a fund holds reports, a heads-up with the fund's position and the stock's typical results-day move. Only for confirmed dates, never Yahoo's estimates (for SGX stocks these are often a guess from last year).
- **Where the dates come from, best first:** US companies' SEC filings (the results release, with the minute it was filed: out after the 4pm close means the next day trades on it), then the date the company confirmed on Yahoo Finance, then Yahoo's estimate. SGX has no free equivalent of the SEC's filings, so SGX dates come from Yahoo, and the market memory checks past SGX news dates against trading volume.
- **Cost:** nothing extra for the data (Yahoo is asked once a day, one request per stock, and the SEC at most every 2 hours); the extra context adds roughly US$0.20 a month to the AI decisions. If a source fails, the last copy is kept, and without any the dates are simply unknown.

**Optional: US results dates from the SEC.** The SEC's EDGAR system is free and official, but it asks every automated client to say who is calling, with a contact email, and blocks those that don't. Add a repository **variable** (Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions → **Variables** tab, not a secret) named **`SEC_USER_AGENT`**, in the form `Your Name your@email.com`, e.g. `Jane Tan jane.tan@example.com`. It's only sent to the SEC, never stored in the code. Actions logs of a public repository are public and show a variable's value in that step's log; if you'd rather the address stayed out of it, add it as a repository **secret** with the same name instead (secrets are hidden in logs). Without it, the SEC step is skipped and Yahoo's dates are used for US stocks too; with it, the first run also reads the past year's filings. Berkshire Hathaway (BRK-B) publishes its results in its quarterly report, so that is the date used for it.

### Stock notes: each stock's own history and risk

The market memory pools DBS with Singapore Airlines. A stock's own notes show that stock's history, as counts, never as rules. Tap a stock's symbol on the Home page (your holdings, or *Stock notes* on an AI pick), on Markets, or under a fund's positions:

- **Its typical day:** the spread of its last 60 days' moves (a stop-loss 1–2 of those away is often hit by ordinary swings), and its beta against its index over the year.
- **The stocks it moves most with:** the correlation of their daily moves over the year. DBS, OCBC and UOB usually move together, so holding all three is close to one bet.
- **Stop-losses:** from ten years of its prices, how often ordinary swings touched a stop 2 or 3 typical daily moves away within 21 trading days, and the stop they reached in only 1 hold in 5 (the *1-in-5 stop*), at today's daily move.
- **Results:** its latest 8 results days against the index (the day the price could react, then a week and a month from the close before, from the results calendar's dates), its typical results-day move, and the next date with where it comes from (an SEC filing, confirmed on Yahoo Finance, or Yahoo's estimate).
- **Dividends:** the last one, how much of the dividend the price usually lost on the ex-date (SGX, from ten years: a stop-loss tighter than that drop can trigger on the dividend alone), the months it usually goes ex, and the next ex-date as an estimate (a year after the one a year before).
- **Its record:** the home page's AI picks on it (after a week and a month: how many were right and beat the index), and each fund's (its trades on it: separate bets and the stock-specific edge a week later, and its other ideas on it).
- **Your note** (admins): one note per stock, shared by every fund. Each fund's position in it shows your note, and every AI fund in its market reads it.

On the AI fund page, **Stops and risk** sets each position against its stock's typical daily move: its share of the fund, how much a typical day's move in it moves the fund (and its share of all of them; an index fund, which has no notes of its own, measured from its prices), its stop-loss (set from the average price) with how far today's price is from that level in typical daily moves, so a position in profit has more room, the 1-in-5 stop, and how far it has gone against and for the fund since it opened (its worst and best move, as its stops see them, from the first price after it was bought, followed every 15 minutes; each exit keeps them, and Telegram's stop-loss and take-profit messages say them; a position from before this tracking began is followed from then, and says so). It warns straight away when risk is uneven (*NVDA is 35% of the fund but 57% of its daily risk*: one position's share of the daily risk at 1.3 times its share of the money or more, and 30% of it, among the positions whose daily move is known), when two positions on the same side moved together (a correlation of 0.7 or more), when the usual ex-date drop would reach a stop's level on its own (also when the stock is later than last year going ex: due any day), and how tight its stop-losses have been set (every new stop is logged with the stock's daily move at the time; a take-profit moved on its own isn't a new stop).

**What the AI sees:** in the market data each market's funds share (cached for the second fund on), cards for at most 6 stocks, chosen by what's true for the whole market in that run: stocks any of its funds holds, then the home page's picks, then stocks in today's news. Each card is 2–3 short lines, e.g. *±1.1%/day; 1-in-5 stop −6.8%/+7.1%. Results vs ES3: −0.9%, +2.7%, −4.2%, +2.2%; next 2026-11-05 (estimate). Ex-dates drop 88% of the dividend; last 0.2081 (1.2%) on 2026-08-10; next ~2026-11-10.* Your notes on its market's stocks go with them. Each position in its own part adds how far today's price is from its stop-loss level in daily moves, a typical day's move in it as a % of the fund, and its 1-in-5 stop. All of it is advice: the hard limits don't change.

**Where it comes from:** once a day (and with each weekly ten-year build) the job writes `state/dossiers.json` from the year of prices, the ten-year memory, the results calendar and the picks' history: prices and news only, never a fund's record, for the page. Each run the job builds the same thing in memory for the AI. Each fund's record on each stock, its worst and best moves and its stop log stay with the fund, and your notes with the funds, so they're private when the funds are.

**Cost:** about 430 tokens a decision (the cards about 200, the positions' numbers about 25 each and the system prompt's explanation about 115): roughly US$0.13 a month at 150 decisions, less when funds decide together and share the cached market data.

These are counts from each stock's own past on today's watchlist, which survived and mostly won: the next results day or ex-date can go either way.

### News feeds: leads for the news digest

The news digest (Claude Haiku searching the web, shared by the picks and the funds) only knows what its few searches find, and Singapore news is thin on the web. So at most every 6 hours the job also reads free news feeds, listed in `feeds.json`:

| Feed | What it carries |
|---|---|
| The Business Times: Companies & Markets | Singapore companies and markets, about 100 headlines |
| The Straits Times: Business | 8 to 12 headlines |
| CNA: Business | 20 headlines, often without a summary |
| The Smart Investor | Singapore investing articles. Cloudflare blocks it at times, so the job doesn't depend on it: its articles also come through Yahoo Finance Singapore |
| CNBC: Top News, Earnings and Technology | 30 headlines each |
| Yahoo Finance headlines, one feed for each stock in `symbols.json` | 5 to 19 headlines each, from Yahoo, Motley Fool, Barchart, TIKR, TheStreet, 24/7 Wall St and others (SGX stocks from Yahoo's Singapore edition). Index funds have none |

Each feed is downloaded on the runner (`scripts/feed_fetch.py`, the same Chrome-like client as Yahoo's prices) and read by `scripts/fetch-articles.mjs` with `articles.js`. The Motley Fool's Singapore site no longer exists and The Edge Singapore blocks the job, so neither is used; nor is Google News.

- **Tagged by what it says:** an item is kept only if its headline, or the start of its summary, names a watchlist stock: by its name in `symbols.json`, its ticker (*AAPL*, *(SGX:U11)*) or a few other names (*DBS*, *OCBC*, *ThaiBev*, *CICT*, *Google*, *Instagram*, `ALIASES` in `articles.js`). Names that mean something else don't count: *Keppel Reit*, *Keppel DC Reit*, *Keppel Infrastructure Trust* and *Keppel Pacific Oak* aren't Keppel; *SIA Engineering* isn't Singapore Airlines; *SGX* in *(SGX:U11)*, *SGX-listed* or *a dual Nasdaq-SGX IPO* isn't Singapore Exchange the company; *CapitaLand Investment* and *CapitaLand Ascendas REIT* aren't CICT; *Apple Hospitality REIT* isn't Apple; and a bank's economists or analysts quoted on other news aren't news about the bank. The feed an item came from doesn't count at all: Yahoo's feed for NVDA carries many stories about other stocks (on the day it was checked, 1 of its 18 headlines was about Nvidia).
- **What's kept:** for each item that names a stock, only its headline (up to 200 characters), link (without tracking parameters such as `utm_…`), site, feed, time and the stocks it names; never an article's text or summary. They go in `state/articles/YYYY-MM.json` on the public `ai-state` branch (public headlines and links; at most 3,000 a month, 13 months kept). The same link, or the same headline within 3 days (one story on two sites or in two feeds), is kept once.
- **Leads for the digest:** the digest gets the 15 freshest tagged headlines about its stocks (none more than 4 days old, the two markets taken in turn so the US feeds' volume doesn't crowd out SGX) as *leads (headlines only)*, with each one's site, age and link. It uses them to decide what to search for, and an item may cite a lead's link, which then passes the check that keeps only links it was actually given (as a page from its searches does), so the picks and the funds can cite it too. Its number of web searches doesn't change. To switch the leads off, add the repository **variable** **`NEWS_LEADS`** = `off` (Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions → Variables); anything else, or nothing, leaves them on.
- **Are the leads worth it?** Each digest's record (how many items, how many kept a checked link, from its searches or a lead, how many came from a lead, its searches and cost: counts only, in `state/news-quality.json`) is printed week by week, with the leads on or off, by **Actions → Market memory report → Run workflow**. Compare a week with the leads off (`NEWS_LEADS` = `off`) against a week with them on before changing the digest's searches: a worse digest wouldn't show in the picks' track record for months. When the funds are private, only the picks' digests are recorded (a fund's own digest would say when it decided).
- **Which big moves had news:** the headlines also tell which big moves against the index had news around them (see *Big moves with and without news* under *How the AI funds learn*).
- **When a feed fails:** each feed has health counters (`state/feed-health.json`). One that fails 3 tries in a row (about a day) is tried once a day until it answers again, and the job's log lists it; a fetch in which no feed answered doesn't count against them (the runner's connection is the likelier fault). The step can fail without affecting anything else: without the feeds, the digest works as it did before, and big moves aren't judged until most feeds answer again.
- **Cost:** the feeds are free. The leads add roughly 900 tokens to each digest (about a tenth of a US cent), and the searches behind big moves without news at most about US$0.20 a month.

### Reading guide: what investing sites said, and your own reading

On 17 stocks you can't tell a writer's or a site's skill from luck for a year or more, so the app keeps no league table of writers or brokers and the AI never sees any of this. Instead, the Home page's **reading guide** is a dated record of what investing sites recommended, graded honestly, for your own reading.

- **The daily read:** once a day (20 hours after the last), right after a fetch of the news feeds, Claude Haiku reads up to 40 of the headlines stored in the last 4 days that it hasn't read yet, investing sites first (The Motley Fool, Barchart, TIKR, TheStreet, 24/7 Wall St, Yahoo Finance Singapore, The Smart Investor), then headlines that read like analysis (a question, a call, a target, or a ticker in brackets, as Yahoo Finance's own analysis pieces write it), then the news. Each is read with the start of its summary, taken from that run's downloads and cut to 300 characters; the summary is used in that call only and never stored. For each stock a headline names, it gives the article's own call (buy, sell, hold or none), the price target it sets (0 for none) and the reasons it cites, through a strict tool; code keeps only a call on a stock listed with that article. A news report, a question the article doesn't answer, or a broker's rating it only reports isn't a call, so no broker is graded.
- **What's kept:** one call per site, per stock, per week (the first), dated when the article was published, with the stock's price then, shaped like the AI picks' history, in `state/reading-calls.json` (public: the sites' headlines and links, which are kept for 3 weeks for the page's list of the latest calls; calls for 400 days, at most 3,000). Holds are kept but not graded: a hold has no direction.
- **Graded like the AI picks:** `scorecard.js` grades each call unchanged: a month (21 trading days) later, in the call's direction, against the same bet on the index (SPY or ES3), dividends included, before fees. Each site's record shows its calls, how many are graded, its separate weeks, how many beat the index, and the average a month later with a likely range (an 8-in-10 chance), counting each calendar week once: calls on the same stock and side within a month are one bet, so a week when several sites called the same move counts once. A chart shows the sites with 5 or more graded calls, and its Table every site.
- **No verdict before 20 graded calls**, and then only when the average is well clear of noise (a 99.8% chance of its sign on a t-distribution over the weeks): *did better than the index so far*, *did worse*, or *no evidence either way yet*. That level was set on made-up sites with no skill at all (`readingNoiseCheck` in `reading.js`, run by the tests): of 1,000 of them, re-checked every week for a year, 3.7% showed a verdict at some point. It's slow to credit real skill too: in the same test, a site making 3 calls a week that really beat the index by 1% a month showed a verdict within the year 1 time in 10, and one beating it by 2% about half the time.
- **Honest limits, which the page repeats:** a site's record takes a year or more to mean anything. There are 17 stocks, a site calls a few of them a month and calls bunch after results, so the same few price moves count again and again. Calls are graded against the index without allowing for beta, so buying stocks that swing more than the index looks good in a rising market. A good record says little about the next call.
- **Your reading:** admins get a *Log an article* box under the guide. Give the link to an article you read (in Tiger, Standard Chartered or anywhere), or paste its text (up to 3,000 characters), for example when it's behind a paywall. It reaches the job through the same passthrough as your notes (a *settings* request, read from the run's event file). If you gave a link, the job downloads the page to the runner (`scripts/page_fetch.py`, the same Chrome-like client as the feeds; only a public web page, never saved) and reads its title, summary, published time and the first 1,500 characters of its paragraphs, never a paywalled body hidden in the page's data. When the page can't be fetched or gives too little (The Smart Investor's pages are blocked, a paywall shows only a teaser), the box says so and keeps the link, so you can paste the text and log it again. The same call finds its calls, and they're graded the same way under *Your reading*, from when you log the article (so an older one isn't graded on moves you'd already seen), with a record of all of them and the same rule of no verdict before 20. The same article isn't logged twice in a week, and the latest 100 are kept. They're stored with the funds (`c.reading`): private with `FUND_STATE_TOKEN`; otherwise on the public `ai-state` branch, like your notes, while the website's copy keeps only how many there are. The Actions log never prints the article's link, site, title, text or calls, and no AI prompt ever sees them.
- **Cost:** the daily read is one Haiku call without web search, about 5,000 tokens in and 1,500 out: roughly 1 to 1.5 US cents a day (the first live read cost US$0.013), about US$0.30–0.40 a month. An article you log costs well under 1 US cent. Both are in the spend ledger as *reading* (the spend chart's *Reading guide*), and both stop at the monthly cap.

### Ask the data: your own questions, checked on held-out years

Admins get an **Ask the data** box on each fund's page, under the ten-year panel. Type a question in plain words (up to 300 characters) about how the watchlist's stocks have behaved, e.g. *Do SGX banks recover after going ex-dividend?*, *Does NVDA keep falling the week after a big drop?*, *After US results that beat, does the stock keep rising for a month?*, *Do stocks that beat the index one week lag it the next?* Each is answered from the same ten years of prices, and with the same checks, as the ten-year market memory (see *How the AI funds learn*).

- **The question becomes a query, never code.** It reaches the job through the same passthrough as your notes (a *settings* request with `{ fund: 'all', ask }`, read from the run's event file). One Claude Haiku call without web search, through a strict tool, turns it into a query in a small fixed language (`hypotheses.js`), or says in one sentence why this data can't answer it (news, valuations, analysts' views, other stocks, prices within the day, a forecast rather than what the past shows). Code then checks the query against a whitelist, and only ever compares its values with fixed lists; nothing in it is run:
  - **what kind of day:** big one-day moves (4% or more and 2.5 times the stock's usual daily move), ex-dividend dates, results days (the first session trading on them), every stock's every week, or, later, the AI funds' own trade ideas;
  - **filters**, where the kind of day takes them: the market or watchlist stocks (a stock not on the watchlist, or an index fund, can't be asked about), the move's direction and size, heavy volume (2x the 50-day average or more), the VIX calm, normal or stressed that day, the index above or below its 200-day average, and results that beat or missed;
  - **over** the next trading day, week or month (21 trading days), and **what you expect**: the price to rise or fall beyond the market, the move (or the news, or the week's move against the index, or the funds' ideas) to continue or reverse, or nothing in particular.
  The page shows how it was read (*Read as: …*), so you can see when it was misread and ask again.
- **The answer** is measured as the ten-year studies are: each case's return beyond the market (its beta times the index) and the stock's own usual drift, the same stock and side close together counting as one bet, uncertainty worked out by date (by week for a month); found on 2016–2023 (a 90% chance of its sign, 8 or more separate bets, and at least 0.3% over its horizon) and then checked on 2024 onwards, which it wasn't found on (the same sign at half its size or more, from 5 or more bets). It's one of:
  - **confirmed**: a pattern that held, the way you expected;
  - **rejected**: *no reliable pattern* (a common and honest answer, as it was for almost every study in the first ten-year build), a pattern that didn't hold on 2024 onwards, or the opposite of what you expected;
  - **not enough data**: too few cases in either period;
  - **can't answer with this data**, with the reason.
  Each answer gives the numbers in plain English: the effect with its likely range, the cases, the separate bets and separate dates, and what the held-out years showed and whether they agreed. An ex-dividend answer also says how much of the dividend the price lost on the day. A stock the ten-year build left out (Thai Beverage, when Yahoo's adjusted prices are broken) is named, with why; if a market's index (SPY or ES3) is the one left out, a question on that market is answered *can't answer* with why, rather than waiting and downloading the ten years again every day. A market whose prices simply didn't arrive is tried again the next day. The same rules on pure noise let through about 1 made-up pattern in 45 (`holdoutNoiseCheck`); ask many questions and expect the occasional false *confirmed*.
- **The funds' own ideas** open to questions only once their graded ideas go back 6 months (before that the answer is *not enough data*, with the date it opens). They're the trades each market's funds made or proposed, pooled, one case per fund, stock and side per 5 trading days, with their edge a week or a month later after beta and fees, and the VIX, the index trend and volume from each idea's factors; found on the first two thirds of the time since the first and checked on the last third. They're answered in the AI fund step as soon as they're asked.
- **When:** the ten years of raw prices are only on the runner while the weekly step downloads them, and your questions are private, in the fund collection, which the job loads after that step. So a step after the AI fund step answers them (`scripts/build-history.mjs asked` and `answer`): from that run's weekly download when there was one, otherwise by downloading the ten years again (the same 21 requests), at most once every 20 hours while a question waits. A question is usually answered in the run that reads it, and always within a day. The ten-year memory itself is still rebuilt only weekly.
- **Kept with the funds** (`c.questions`, the newest 30, about 1 KB each): private with `FUND_STATE_TOKEN`; otherwise on the public `ai-state` branch, like your notes, while the website's copy keeps only how many there are. The Actions log and the app's messages never show a question, its reading or its answer (only counts), and no AI prompt of the funds or the picks ever sees them.
- **Cost:** about 0.3 US cents a question (the spend chart's *Ask the data*, task *ask*), stopping at the monthly cap. The answers cost nothing.
- **Not built:** the optional quarterly call that would have a stronger model propose its own questions from tables of 2016–2022. Only your questions are answered.

### Decisions per trading day

How often a fund asks the AI what to do: **1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 times a trading day, or at every run** (about every 15 minutes while its market trades, about 26 a day). Set it when starting a fund and change it any time under *Mandate, approval and limits*. The trading day, from 15 minutes after the open to the close by the clock, is cut into that many equal parts, and the fund decides at the first run in each, so a day never has more than you chose; a part that falls wholly in SGX's lunch break (12:00–13:00) is skipped, so 16 a day on SGX is 15. Stop-losses and take-profits are checked at every run whatever you choose.

Each decision is one AI call, about US$0.05–0.15 on the default model, so this sets most of a fund's cost: roughly US$1–3 a month at 1 a day, US$17–50 at 16, and US$27–82 at every run, or about a third of that with *Save AI cost: skip a decision when nothing has changed* on (it skips up to two quiet runs in a row). Every fund shares the monthly cap (US$30 unless you change `AI_MONTHLY_CAP_USD`), and reaching it stops all the scheduled AI, picks included, for the rest of the month, so raise the cap before choosing every run. A cheaper AI model for the fund (same panel) cuts the cost further: Claude Haiku 4.5 costs about half as much a decision as the default Sonnet 5 (Opus 5 about 2.5 times), and the costs shown beside each choice follow the model picked. Haiku's judgement is weaker; leaving another fund on the default model shows on the leaderboard, after AI cost, whether the saving is worth it.

### Starting, stopping or replacing the AI fund

**From the app (accounts on, admins only):** the AI fund tab has *Start AI fund* and *Stop fund* buttons. They need a one-time setup, because Supabase starts the GitHub workflow for you:

1. In GitHub, go to **Settings → Developer settings → Personal access tokens → Fine-grained tokens → Generate new token**. Choose *Only select repositories → paper-trader*, and under *Repository permissions* set **Actions** to **Read and write**.
2. In Supabase's **SQL Editor**, run `supabase/fund-control.sql`.
3. Still in the SQL Editor, store the token in Supabase's encrypted Vault:
   `select vault.create_secret('github_pat_…your token…', 'github_actions_token');`

The token never reaches the browser. Only admins can send start or stop requests, and the fund appears or changes within about 3–5 minutes.

**From GitHub:** go to **Actions → Update prices, AI picks and AI fund → Run workflow** and fill in:

- *Start a NEW AI fund with this amount*: e.g. `10000`. It runs alongside any others (balanced style; use the app to choose a style, focus and model).
- *New fund's currency*: `USD` (US stocks) or `SGD` (SGX stocks).
- *How many times a trading day the AI decides*: 1, 2 or 4. (From the app you can also choose 8, 16 or every run, and change it on a running fund; see *Decisions per trading day*.)
- *Stop the AI fund*: closes all its positions and stops it (only when one fund is running; with several, use the app).

### Trading through Tiger Brokers

The AI fund can trade through your Tiger Brokers account instead of the simulator. Start with Tiger's **paper account** (simulated money) and approval on.

**How it works:** each run, a Python step (`scripts/tiger_broker.py`, using Tiger's official `tigeropen` library) checks your open orders and fills with Tiger. The fund then decides what to do, and a second step sends any new orders.
- **Orders:** always DAY limit orders, at most 1% worse than the latest price, and only while the market is actually trading.
- **Fills:** the fund records Tiger's actual fill prices.
- **Approval (the default):** the AI's trades wait under *Waiting for your approval* until you tap **Approve**, or **Reject**, which asks why (see *Your calls* under *How the AI funds learn*). A proposal expires after an hour, or if the price moves more than 2% first. Stop-losses and take-profits don't wait, because they reduce risk.
- **Stop-losses held by Tiger:** each stop-loss is also placed at Tiger as a standing (good-till-cancelled) stop order, so it triggers straight away even if GitHub runs the job late or not at all. Shorts always get one at the 40% forced-cover level. When the position changes size, the stop order is replaced; before any other order sells the same shares, the stop order is cancelled first, so shares are never sold twice. If Tiger refuses a stop order, the app's own 15-minute check covers that stock instead.
- **Limits, enforced in code:** the fund's budget (open orders count as spent), a maximum size per order, and a daily-loss limit that pauses the fund.
- **Kill switch:** **Pause all trading** stops new trades and cancels open orders.
- **Mismatch check:** if the fund's positions and your Tiger account disagree, the page warns you.

**Setup:**
1. **Get Open API access from Tiger.** On a computer, log in at [developer.tigerbrokers.com.sg](https://developer.tigerbrokers.com.sg/) (it isn't in the phone app) and register as a developer. The *Developer Information* page then shows your **Tiger ID**, **License** and **Demo Account** (the 17-digit paper account). Under *Your RSA KEY*, click **Regenerate** and copy the **PKCS#1 private key** (for Python) straight away: Tiger shows it only once.
2. **Add GitHub repository secrets** (Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions → Secrets):
   - `TIGEROPEN_TIGER_ID`: your Tiger ID;
   - `TIGEROPEN_ACCOUNT`: your **paper** account number to start with;
   - `TIGEROPEN_PRIVATE_KEY`: the private key text;
   - `TIGEROPEN_LICENSE`: `TBSG` for Tiger Brokers Singapore.
3. **Check the connection:** Actions → *Check Tiger connection* → **Run workflow**. It only reads (no orders) and should print `Connected to Tiger: paper account ending ...` with the demo balance. Balances are printed for paper accounts only, since Actions logs of a public repo are public.
4. **Re-run `supabase/fund-control.sql`** in Supabase's SQL Editor, so the app can send approvals, pause and settings.
5. **Start the fund:** in the app, go to *AI fund → Start AI fund → Trades go to: Tiger Brokers account*, keeping *I approve each trade*. Tiger's paper account may not cover SGX, so start with a USD fund.

**Going live with real money** takes two deliberate changes. Change `TIGEROPEN_ACCOUNT` to your live account number, **and** add the repository variable `TIGER_LIVE_TRADING` = `yes` (Variables tab). Without the variable, orders for a live account are refused. The page shows *Tiger LIVE account (real money)* in red when it's live.

**Privacy:** the website is public. Its copy of the fund leaves out your Tiger account's full positions and Tiger's order numbers, but the fund's own trades and value are visible to anyone with the link.

### Keeping the AI fund private

By default the AI fund's trades, value and positions are on the public website and the public `ai-state` branch, and its trades appear in the public Actions logs. To keep them private (only admins see the fund, in the app, after signing in):

1. In Supabase → **SQL Editor**, run `supabase/private-fund.sql`. Its last line shows a long random token.
2. Add that token as a GitHub repository secret named **`FUND_STATE_TOKEN`**.

From the next run, the fund is stored in Supabase instead (readable only by admins), the website stops publishing it, the `ai-state` branch is restarted without it (its old history is gone), and the Actions logs stop printing its trades. Your notes on stocks, your reasons for declining trades, the articles you log (*Your reading*), your questions (*Ask the data*) and the weekly reports are stored with the funds too: without this they're on the public `ai-state` branch, where the website's copy leaves out your notes' words (keeping only when each was saved, so the page can't show them back to you), your logged articles and your questions (keeping only how many of each), your reasons for declining (so *Your calls* shows only in Telegram's weekly report) and all but the latest 4 reports; with it they're private, and the page shows them. Your notes and reasons reach the job in the workflow's `fund_command` input, which the job reads from the run's event file on the runner, so the public Actions log never prints them (a step's environment would be printed), with or without this. If Supabase can't be reached, the fund skips that run rather than working from an old copy; if a save fails, that run's copy goes on the branch so no Tiger order is ever forgotten, and moves back to Supabase next run. Invited members who aren't admins no longer see the fund.

### Telegram alerts

Get a Telegram message when trades are waiting for your approval (with the deadline and a link to that fund's page, where you approve or decline them), orders fill, stop-losses or take-profits trigger, Tiger refuses an order, the fund pauses, an AI decision fails, the monthly AI cap is reached, the fund and your Tiger account disagree, a request you sent from the app doesn't work, or a dividend or split lands, plus a short summary after each trading day's close (value, the day's change, the index and the AI cost), a heads-up the evening before a stock a fund holds reports its results (confirmed dates only), and a line when the catalyst a held position was opened for has passed, with what the AI expected, how it's doing so far and what would prove it wrong. Each fund's weekly report comes as a message of its own, after its market's last session of the week, with a link that opens that fund's page at the report. A link to a fund you've since removed says so on the page, rather than opening another fund's report.

1. In Telegram, message **@BotFather**, send `/newbot`, and pick a name. It replies with a **token**.
2. Add it as a GitHub repository secret named **`TELEGRAM_BOT_TOKEN`**.
3. In Telegram, open your new bot and press **Start**.
4. In GitHub, go to **Actions → Set up Telegram alerts → Run workflow**. Your bot messages you your **chat id**.
5. Add it as a secret named **`TELEGRAM_CHAT_ID`**, then run *Set up Telegram alerts* again for a test message.

Alerts are checked on every scheduled run (every 15 minutes while markets trade). Their content never appears in the Actions log. The message links to the app (`#fund/<id>` opens that fund's page; if you have to sign in first, you still end up there), where you approve or decline trades.

### Which model does what

The work is split by difficulty to keep costs down:

| Task | Model | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Searching the web and summarising the news, looking up the news behind a big move without any, reading the calls in articles for the reading guide, and turning your *Ask the data* questions into queries | **Claude Haiku 4.5** (cheapest) | Simple but reads a lot: search results are most of the tokens. |
| AI picks, AI strategist, AI fund trades | **Claude Sonnet 5** | Real judgement, but it only reads Haiku's short news summary and the price statistics. |

One news summary per run is shared by the picks and the AI fund. To change the models, add repository variables (**Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions → Variables**): `AI_NEWS_MODEL` for the news step and `AI_MODEL` for the decisions (`claude-haiku-4-5`, `claude-sonnet-5` or `claude-opus-5`). In the app, *Settings → Model for picks and strategies* does the same for what you run in the browser.

The funds' weekly review runs on the news model. `AI_REVIEW_MODEL` (optional) moves it to a stronger model, but only once the fund's market has 150 graded ideas across its funds (an idea repeated, or made by two funds, counts once): until then the number of ideas, not the model, limits what a review can find, and a stronger model would only write noise more convincingly, so the job keeps the cheaper one and says so in its log. It costs about twice as much per review.

### Costs

A picks refresh (news plus picks) typically costs about US$0.10–0.30, and an AI fund decision about US$0.05–0.15 when it reuses that news. Web searches are US$0.01 each. The news feeds are free, and the searches behind big moves without news cost at most about US$0.20 a month (10 searches); the spend chart shows them as *article look-ups*. The reading guide's daily read costs roughly US$0.25–0.30 a month (the spend chart's *Reading guide*). A question to *Ask the data* costs about 0.3 US cents. The picks refresh about twice a day, and a new fund makes 1 decision a day unless you choose more, so expect well under US$1 per trading day. For comparison, a picks refresh done entirely on Opus 5 cost US$1.50. Every AI result in the app shows its approximate cost.

**Monthly cap:** the scheduled AI (picks and the AI fund) stops for the rest of the month once its estimated spend reaches **US$30**. Stop-losses keep working, and the fund page and your Telegram say so. Change the cap with the repository variable `AI_MONTHLY_CAP_USD` (`0` means no cap). *Settings → Connections* shows this month's spend, and what this browser has spent with your own key. The ledger (`ai-spend.json`) adds to a hundredth of a cent, so calls that cost a fraction of a cent (a question, a logged article) add up and count toward the cap; the page rounds to cents.

GitHub turns off scheduled workflows in a public repo after 60 days with no commits. The `ai-state` commits count toward that, but if everything stops, re-enable the workflow on the Actions tab.

## Adding or removing stocks

Edit `symbols.json` and push. Use Yahoo Finance tickers: SGX stocks end in `.SI` (DBS is `D05.SI`), and US stocks are plain (`AAPL`, `BRK-B`). Mark index funds with `"etf": true` (they have no results or analysts), and give a US company its SEC number as `"cik"` (10 digits, from [sec.gov/search-filings](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings)) for its results dates from filings. The news feeds follow `symbols.json` by themselves (Yahoo's headline feed for each stock) and tag a stock by its name there and its ticker; if it's known by other names, or its name is part of other companies' (as Keppel's is of Keppel Reit), add them to `ALIASES` in `articles.js`, with a test in `test/articles.test.mjs`.
